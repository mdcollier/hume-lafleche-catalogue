Where the two texts meet and part

AI-assisted summary (written for display from one model analyst's reading; not reviewed by a second reader; not human-verified)

Régis, paragraphs p513 to p532 of the machine-read text, is one compact apologetic argument: a rule for believing attested facts, applied to Moses and then to prophecy and the apostles, ending in the claim that religion needs only historical proof together with the gift of faith. The same joints appear in Hume's section in a different order: the rule of evidence, the supposition of fraud or folly, the Pentateuch, prophecy, omnipotence, faith. The two texts share a framework (facts are contingent, testimony gives less than sense, the question is the credit of the reporters) and are opposed on whether the nature of the fact counts against the testimony. That weighing is the part of Hume's section with no counterpart in the Régis stretch.

Pattern. Many-to-many and mostly oppositional. Régis: rule (p513), Moses (p519–526), prophecy and the apostles (p527–528), method and limits (p530–532). Hume: the rule of evidence, the causes of false report, the limits, then Moses and prophecy. One Hume paragraph, ¶40, meets six Régis paragraphs.

What this does not show

The analyst rated eleven of the thirteen links at partial or better as common to the seventeenth-century genre of proving Christianity from attested facts, the other two as undetermined, and none as specific to Régis. The reading shows that a book recorded in the catalogue of the college library carries, in one stretch, an argument of the kind Hume's section examines. It does not show that Hume had this or any particular book in view.

L1 p513 EHU 10 ¶5, ¶8 qualification partial common to genre or tradition Matters of fact carry no necessity, so belief on testimony is a posteriori. Régis denies a necessary link between facts and the nature of things; Hume denies one between a report and the event.
L2 p513, p514 EHU 10 ¶8, ¶36 qualification substantive common to genre or tradition The rule for sufficient attestation. Régis's Axiom 1 looks only at the witnesses (number, times, nations, interests, first-hand, no collusion) and equates such testimony with eyesight. Hume grants a rule of that kind for the eight-day darkness, with a term absent from Régis's rule: the unusualness of the fact diminishes the testimony.
L3 p519, p524 EHU 10 ¶5, ¶37 opposition substantive undetermined Testimony rests on a constancy in human nature; may mass folly be supposed? Régis makes the supposition of actual madness in a great number the limit of historical certainty. Hume shares the ground (the delirious man has no authority) but holds fraud and credulity common enough to be preferred to a violation of nature.
L4 p525 EHU 10 ¶13, ¶40 opposition substantive common to genre or tradition The falsehood of the record treated as itself a miracle, said of the same book. Régis: it would be a kind of miracle had the Jews accepted the Law from a man who did nothing extraordinary. Hume: the falsehood must be more miraculous than the miracles related, and for the Pentateuch it is not.
L5 p519, p520, p525 EHU 10 ¶40 opposition substantive common to genre or tradition The conditions of the Pentateuch's attestation, point against point: universal corroboration versus none; contemporaneous writing versus composition long after; the people as interested guardians versus the people as the author's partial countrymen.
L6 p523 EHU 10 ¶22, ¶23 qualification substantive undetermined A rule about impostors: they avoid audiences that are hard to deceive. Shared major premise; the minor premise is opposite (Régis: 600,000 watchful men; Hume ¶40: the opposite character of the same people).
L7 p523 EHU 10 ¶27 opposition partial common to genre or tradition Publicity and hostile witnesses as a mark of truth. Régis treats a whole people of enemies as witnesses no impostor would choose; Hume gives a case with learned, hostile examiners and still rejects it. Hume's case is not Régis's.
L8 p527, p528 EHU 10 ¶13, ¶17 opposition substantive common to genre or tradition The deceived-or-deceiver dilemma applied to a report of a resurrection. Régis closes both horns; Hume makes the dilemma one side of a balance against the fact itself and treats both horns as open (enthusiasm, pious fraud).
L9 p527, p531 EHU 10 ¶41 opposition partial common to genre or tradition Fulfilled prophecy as a proof of revelation. Régis counts it as an independent attested fact; Hume subsumes it under miracle and applies the same test.
L10 p530, p531, p532 EHU 10 ¶35, ¶40 opposition substantive common to genre or tradition The programme of grounding the Christian religion on attested facts by the principles of historical certainty. Régis states and commends it; Hume denies that human testimony can make a miracle the foundation of a religion and tests the Pentateuch on those terms.
L11 p531 EHU 10 ¶1, ¶41 qualification partial common to genre or tradition Historical proof is insufficient for belief without a supernatural operation. Régis says so sincerely (the gift of faith); Hume says so ironically (faith as itself a miracle) and reports Tillotson's exception for the Holy Spirit.
L12 p532 EHU 10 ¶38 opposition partial common to genre or tradition The appeal to divine omnipotence. Régis: God's will is the rule of the possible, so nothing he does is impossible. Hume: ascribing the event to the Almighty does not make it more probable. Régis argues possibility, Hume probability.
L13 p513, p537 EHU 10 ¶1 support partial common to genre or tradition Testimony yields a lower certainty than sense. Régis holds both premises of the argument Hume reports from Tillotson (religion's proof is testimonial; testimony is only morally certain) without drawing its conclusion.
L14 p523 EHU 10 ¶36 different target topical undetermined A days-long darkness as the prodigy under discussion. No shared argument; possibly coincidence.

The analyst's statement of the relation (Régis p513, p530)

AI-assisted reading (one model analyst, one pass; not human-verified)

Opposition over the rule of evidence for testimony. Régis's Axiom 1 makes the credit of testimony depend on properties of the witnesses alone and treats miracle reports as ordinary truths of fact, excluding examination of what is attested because divine will is the measure of possibility. Hume's ¶8 has a term absent from Régis's rule (evidence diminishes as the fact is unusual), ¶38 addresses an appeal to omnipotence, ¶37 denies the premise that mass folly or knavery is incredible, and ¶40 applies the weighing to the Pentateuch read as human history, which is also Régis's test case.
