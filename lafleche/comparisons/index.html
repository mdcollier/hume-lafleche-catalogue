The analyst's statement of the relation (Régis p210)

AI-assisted reading (one model analyst, one pass; not human-verified)

Régis p209-p210 is a textbook instance of the definition of judgment (separating or uniting two ideas, every proposition having two terms) that Hume states and rejects in THN 1.3.7 ¶5 as the common doctrine of the logicians. Hume opposes it on two grounds present in the section: existential propositions contain only one idea, and uniting or separating ideas is available to mere conception and so cannot constitute assent. Both authors agree that conceiving the terms is not sufficient; they differ on what is added (Régis: an act of binding that is the affirming; Hume: a manner of conception). The opposition runs through a generic target; nothing in the text ties Hume to Régis in particular.

Régis and Hume share a two-part step: a sensation as sensed cannot be false, and referring it to an external object is a further mental act not chargeable to the senses. They put it to opposed uses. No shared argument. Régis separates a present sensation from the object now producing it, assigns that attribution to judgment rather than sense, and uses the point to show that faith (the Eucharist) cannot contradict the senses and that natural evidence is certain. Both passages state substantially the same law of machines (unequal weights or forces balanced or overmatched by trading force against velocity) and both make geometry instrumental to a physical law. They differ on the law's status: Régis presents it as a necessary consequence of a known mechanical cause of weight and as the true cause of all equilibrium; Hume presents it as a law discovered merely by experience, with ultimate causes out of reach. AI-assisted reading (one model analyst, one pass; not human-verified)

Buffier (p247, with p249 as its proviso) and Hume (THN 1.4.3 ¶9) share a three-place ranking in which an over-refined philosophy stands below common opinion and sound philosophy ends beside common opinion. The content is inverted. Buffier's sound philosophy holds to mankind's uniform judgment because it is true, and p249 protects that inference by excluding quasi-universal errors bred by prejudice and passion. Hume's true philosophy declares the vulgar opinion false and shares with the vulgar only indifference. Buffier's condemned philosophers are sceptical deniers of principles; Hume's are dogmatic seekers of hidden powers, and his true philosophers are moderate sceptics.
