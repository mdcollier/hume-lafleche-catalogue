The analyst's statement of the relation (Crousaz p67, p78, p165)

AI-assisted reading (one model analyst, one pass; not human-verified)

Crousaz and Hume share a negative premise: the senses deliver only the perception, and the ascription of an outer object standing to it as cause and likeness is a further mental act, not a datum of sense. They then diverge: Crousaz assigns that act to judgment and the understanding and holds it justifiable (the senses even furnish proofs of the existence of surrounding objects), whereas Hume denies it to both senses and reason and assigns the belief to the imagination. Crousaz's three-faculty division is a taxonomy of the sources of ideas and is not run as an elimination on external-world belief.

The analyst's statement of the relation (Crousaz p110, p204)

AI-assisted reading (one model analyst, one pass; not human-verified)

Crousaz ranks the pure understanding above sense and imagination because it depends less on the body, and derives a rule that the ideas of sense and imagination are to be regulated and corrected by those of the understanding, whose ideas never destroy one another (p110, p111, p114, p115). Hume grants that the flights of imagination are dangerous (1.4.7 ¶6) but rejects the resolution to adhere to the understanding, because the understanding acting alone subverts itself (¶7), having already founded memory, senses and understanding on the imagination's vivacity (¶3). The relation is concessive opposition to a rule of correction, with an attack on its stated ground. It is an opposition to a type of doctrine of which Crousaz is one instance, not to anything peculiar to Crousaz.

AI-assisted reading (one model analyst, one pass; not human-verified)

The nominated pair shares only its subject, love and hatred. Crousaz (p726) says these passions extend to whatever is related to their principal object and treats this as a cause of unjust, prejudiced judgment; Hume (THN 2.2.6 ¶3) says love and hatred are conjoined with benevolence and anger and that this distinguishes them from pride and humility. Neither argues the other's point. The philosophically significant source text is p723, which grounds the spread of a feeling in the succession of ideas; its natural Hume counterpart is his doctrine of the transfer of passions along related ideas, which lies outside the paragraphs read.

The analyst's statement of the relation (Crousaz p1237)

AI-assisted reading (one model analyst, one pass; not human-verified)

Crousaz and Hume share two premises: enjoyment of an amusement requires attention, and games of skill exercise the same faculty of attention as intellectual work. They put these to different uses. Crousaz, writing a regimen of study, infers that fatiguing games compete with study and are no relaxation for men of letters; Hume, explaining the love of truth, infers that gaming and philosophy please in the same way. They diverge locally on whether a fatiguing or painful engagement can count as amusement, and on why difficulty pleases (vanity for Crousaz, exercise of genius for Hume).
