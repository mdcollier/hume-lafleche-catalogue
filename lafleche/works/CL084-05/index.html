The analyst's statement of the relation (Régis p210)

AI-assisted reading (one model analyst, one pass; not human-verified)

Régis p209-p210 is a textbook instance of the definition of judgment (separating or uniting two ideas, every proposition having two terms) that Hume states and rejects in THN 1.3.7 ¶5 as the common doctrine of the logicians. Hume opposes it on two grounds present in the section: existential propositions contain only one idea, and uniting or separating ideas is available to mere conception and so cannot constitute assent. Both authors agree that conceiving the terms is not sufficient; they differ on what is added (Régis: an act of binding that is the affirming; Hume: a manner of conception). The opposition runs through a generic target; nothing in the text ties Hume to Régis in particular.

The analyst's statement of the relation (Régis p513, p530)

AI-assisted reading (one model analyst, one pass; not human-verified)

Opposition over the rule of evidence for testimony. Régis's Axiom 1 makes the credit of testimony depend on properties of the witnesses alone and treats miracle reports as ordinary truths of fact, excluding examination of what is attested because divine will is the measure of possibility. Hume's ¶8 has a term absent from Régis's rule (evidence diminishes as the fact is unusual), ¶38 addresses an appeal to omnipotence, ¶37 denies the premise that mass folly or knavery is incredible, and ¶40 applies the weighing to the Pentateuch read as human history, which is also Régis's test case.

The analyst's statement of the relation (Régis p647)

AI-assisted reading (one model analyst, one pass; not human-verified)

Regis (p648-649, the paragraphs following the nominated one) argues that the idea of God cannot be an artificial idea produced by mixing or augmenting other ideas and is therefore natural to the mind; Hume (THN 1.4.5 ¶31) asserts, as one premise in a reductio of the Cartesian criterion of causation, that the idea of the supreme Being is derived from particular impressions none of which contains efficacy. This is opposition on the origin of the idea of God. The nominated paragraph p647 itself, on how a finite idea represents an infinite object, has no counterpart in Hume's paragraph.

AI-assisted reading (one model analyst, one pass; not human-verified)

Régis (p657-658) makes God the first cause and external objects the second, immediate causes of ideas, and stipulates that the ordinary way of speaking, on which objects produce ideas, means only this. Hume's section on necessary connexion describes and rejects the kind of position this is: efficacy placed in the deity, in second causes, or in their concurrence (THN 1.3.14 ¶9, ¶11, ¶23). The opposition is real but generic; it is not specific to Régis and does not address the causation of ideas as such.

The analyst's statement of the relation (Régis p777)

AI-assisted reading (one model analyst, one pass; not human-verified)

Régis and Hume share a two-part step: a sensation as sensed cannot be false, and referring it to an external object is a further mental act not chargeable to the senses. They put it to opposed uses. Régis uses it to vindicate the faculties and make error a voluntary overstepping by judgment; Hume uses it to show that the unavoidable belief in distinct existence has no sensory or rational source and is owed to the imagination.

Régis p781 EHU 4 ¶16 model-proposed, awaiting assessment AI-assisted reading (one model analyst, one pass; not human-verified)Passage level topical partial different target EHU 4 ¶21differs from the nominated passage The Cartesian account of error (each faculty infallible within its proper object; the senses deliver only sensations; reference of sensations to external causes belongs to judgment), together with its use to reconcile the Eucharist with the evidence of the senses and to separate the domains of natural evidence and faith.
The analyst's statement of the relation (Régis p781)

AI-assisted reading (one model analyst, one pass; not human-verified)

No shared argument. Régis separates a present sensation from the object now producing it, assigns that attribution to judgment rather than sense, and uses the point to show that faith (the Eucharist) cannot contradict the senses and that natural evidence is certain. Hume separates present sensible qualities from the powers on which future effects depend, denies that either sense or reason reaches those powers, and uses the point to show that inference from experience is not founded on reasoning. What they share is a thin and commonplace premise, that sense is confined to presented qualities; the two bread cases (whiteness of the consecrated host; nourishing power of ordinary bread) coincide only in the noun.

Régis p825 EHU 8 ¶21 selected by the principal investigator as warranting investigation (recorded classification: strong)principal investigator's relation type: doctrinal contrast AI-assisted reading (one model analyst, one pass; not human-verified)Passage level partial substantive opposition EHU 8 ¶21 Cartesian mechanical explanation in physics: the effects of a body are to follow intelligibly, by deduction, from the size, figure and arrangement of its parts, so that a necessary relation between structure and effect is clearly perceived.
The analyst's statement of the relation (Régis p825)

AI-assisted reading (one model analyst, one pass; not human-verified)

Régis's method of physical hypotheses presupposes that the mind can clearly perceive a necessary relation between a supposed size, figure and arrangement of insensible parts and the production of a body's effects. Hume denies that the mind perceives any connexion in the operations of matter beyond constant conjunction and customary inference, and challenges those who claim more to define it and point it out. The two stand in thesis-level opposition on the intelligibility of bodily causation. The opposition is hedged on Régis's side (the configuration is guessed from effects and the science is only probable) and is not argued in either paragraph.

putative zone: Régis and Hume on infinite divisibility
The analyst's statement of the relation (Régis p920)

AI-assisted reading (one model analyst, one pass; not human-verified)

Régis p920 affirms that every finite part of matter contains infinitely many possible parts and defends this by the claim that infinitely many aliquant or proportional parts can be conceived in a quantity without the quantity being conceived infinite. Hume's THN 1.2.2 ¶2 denies that a finite extension can contain infinitely many parts and, in the same paragraph, reports and rejects as frivolous exactly a proportional-parts defence. The two also share a premise: an extended part cannot be indivisible by nature (Hume on physical points, THN 1.2.4 ¶3).

Régis p965 THN 1.3.9 ¶10 model-proposed, awaiting assessment AI-assisted reading (one model analyst, one pass; not human-verified)Passage level topical partial different target THN 1.3.9 ¶10 Cartesian demonstrative physics of collision: rules of impact derived from impenetrability, a least-change principle for incompatible modes, and metaphysical axioms.
The analyst's statement of the relation (Régis p965)

AI-assisted reading (one model analyst, one pass; not human-verified)

p965 and THN 1.3.9 ¶10 share the subject of the communication of motion but not a question. Régis says that distinguishing quantity of motion from speed makes it easy to conceive that a body can give motion to one that has as much or more; he takes for granted that collision transfers motion. Hume asks whether any transfer on impact can be inferred without experience, and explains the contrary illusion by resemblance. The surrounding Régis chapters (p959-p961, p966-p967, p970-p972) show a demonstrative collision physics that Hume's conceivability argument opposes in type, but that is not what p965 says.

Régis p1351 EHU 4 ¶13 model-proposed, awaiting assessment AI-assisted reading (one model analyst, one pass; not human-verified)Passage level partial substantive opposition EHU 4 ¶13 Cartesian mechanics and statics: Descartes's general principle of statics, grounded by Régis in a mechanical (second-element) cause of weight, within a physics that claims to derive laws of motion and to identify true causes.
The analyst's statement of the relation (Régis p1351)

AI-assisted reading (one model analyst, one pass; not human-verified)

Both passages state substantially the same law of machines (unequal weights or forces balanced or overmatched by trading force against velocity) and both make geometry instrumental to a physical law. They differ on the law's status: Régis presents it as a necessary consequence of a known mechanical cause of weight and as the true cause of all equilibrium; Hume presents it as a law discovered merely by experience, with ultimate causes out of reach. Régis's necessity criterion concerns the link between a principle and the truths that depend on it, is applied by observed presence and absence, and is not the a priori cause-effect connexion Hume attacks. The Régis text read does not say how the law is known.
