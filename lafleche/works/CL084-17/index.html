What was found

6 comparisons with Hume are held (3 selected by the principal investigator as warranting investigation, 3 read in context); 3 zones recorded by the principal investigator.

AI-assisted synthesis, written for display from three model analysts' contextual readings (one per comparison); not independently verified by a person

Read in context, all three comparisons were rated partial at passage level and substantive at doctrine level. Two start from a shared premise and diverge. From the premise that a finite mind cannot hold an infinity of parts, Malebranche concludes that the idea of extension is an infinite object distinct from the mind's perception, and Hume that ideas of extension have finitely many indivisible parts. From the separation of what experience delivers from a causal reading of it, Malebranche argues from the idea of body and Hume denies that cause and effect can be settled a priori. In the third, they are opposed on whether God can be conceived without existing. In two, the analyst preferred another paragraph of Hume.

AI-assisted reading (one model analyst, one pass; not human-verified)

Both authors accept that a finite mind cannot hold an infinity of parts as its own content and that stepwise division of finite ideas never completes. Malebranche's Theodore infers that, since the mind nonetheless sees at once that intelligible extension has no unit and no bounds, the idea is an infinite object distinct from the mind's finite perception. Hume infers that, since ideas are the mind's own, ideas of extension consist of a finite number of indivisible parts and geometrical proofs of infinite divisibility fail. The relation is opposition from shared premises.

AI-assisted reading (one model analyst, one pass; not human-verified)

Opposition on whether the thought of God can be had without God's existing. Malebranche (p98, p103-104, presupposed by the nominated p102) holds that no finite idea represents the infinite, so the infinite is seen only in itself and, unlike any creature, cannot be thought without existing. Hume holds that conceiving any object as existent adds no idea to it, so the inseparability Malebranche reserves for God holds trivially of everything and proves nothing; and thinking of God and believing him existent differ only in manner of conception. A secondary contact: Theodore denies that felt force measures the reality of ideas (p111-112), while Hume makes felt force constitutive of belief (paragraphs 5 and 7).

AI-assisted reading (one model analyst, one pass; not human-verified)

Both authors separate the correspondence that experience delivers from a causal reading of it, and both find that a body considered in itself fixes no determinate effect. They then diverge: Theodore argues from the clear idea of body that body-body action is contradictory, whereas Hume holds that nothing about cause and effect can be settled a priori, since every outcome is conceivable and the conceivable implies no contradiction. Hume's nominated paragraph itself concerns projection from past to future, which the source passage does not address.
