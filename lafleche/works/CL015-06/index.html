AI-assisted reading (one model analyst, one pass; not human-verified)

The nominated paragraphs share one premise (repeating a unit is adding, and adding augments) but not an argument. Fenelon uses it to exclude the infinite from number and plurality (the idea of the infinite at p245; the unity of God at p660). Hume uses it to show that a finite extension cannot contain infinitely many parts. The inferences run on different middle terms (non-augmentability of the infinite; summation of non-zero minima), concern different objects, and presuppose different conceptions of the infinite (a simple non-quantitative perfection; the product of endless addition). A closer relation holds between p660-661 and Hume's paragraph 3: both tie being or existence to unity and treat a composite as a number rather than a true unity, applied in opposite directions.

AI-assisted reading (one model analyst, one pass; not human-verified)

The nominated pair (p297 with THN 1.4.5 ¶35) shares a subject but no argument: Hume's ¶35 denies that any a priori conclusion about operations or duration follows for a simple or a compounded substance, while Fénelon's unity argument (p292-p301) draws no conclusion about duration or immortality and is aimed at the non-sensory origin of the idea of unity and at God as the perfectly one being. A narrower real relationship holds with ¶31-32: Fénelon infers a superior being from the heterogeneity of thinking being and bodies and from their mutual sway (p296, p300), and concludes that the idea of perfect unity is innate and is the idea of God (p301); Hume attacks the recourse to a deity grounded on the absence of an apparent connexion between objects, holds that the idea of the supreme Being derives from particular impressions, and concludes that by the mere ideas anything may cause anything.

The analyst's statement of the relation (Fénelon p584)

AI-assisted reading (one model analyst, one pass; not human-verified)

Fénelon refutes a pantheist hypothesis by a dilemma: a whole that is only its parts changes with them (reply 1°, the nominated p584), and a whole that is really one and simple makes contraries coincide (reply 2°, p586). Hume ¶18 reports Spinoza's doctrine on the simple horn, so p584 does not meet it as argument; the two paragraphs share only the disputed thesis of an unchanging one beneath changing diversity. The specific argumentative contact is between p586 and Hume's third reported objection (¶25): incompatible figures, round and square, in one simple subject. Hume grants that objection against Spinoza and redirects it against the theologians' simple soul.

The analyst's statement of the relation (Fénelon p643, p717)

AI-assisted reading (one model analyst, one pass; not human-verified)

No shared argument. Fénelon denies infinite magnitude (and infinite being) to whatever is divisible, taking divisibility as a given, in order to deny that God's eternity and immensity are an endless succession or a boundless space. Hume denies that a finite extension or duration has infinitely many parts, arguing from the finite capacity of the mind. These are different infinities (by extent versus by division) and the two theses are logically independent. The authors are in fact opposed on where indivisibility belongs: for Fénelon it marks the infinite being, for Hume it is the ultimate constituent of finite quantity.
